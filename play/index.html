01 · The idea

One snap, then a trick every 3 seconds.

The Switch 2 is known for one gesture: the Joy-Con snap on with magnets. So the spot opens and closes on that snap, and in between every block shows a different trick, each one about something the console really does: the screen, 120 Hz, detaching, what's inside, the mouse.

It is a spec piece built by the Lab from a real, open-source CAD model. The game on the screen is our own pixel world, because Nintendo's games and characters are not ours to show.

02 · References

What Claude needed before the first prompt.

What people watch

Nintendo's own Switch 2 first-look trailer (27.7M views) is built on the magnetic snap, and the fan remakes of that grammar still pull 1–2M views. Short, satisfying physics loops do even better. So: the snap first, physics you can feel, nothing longer than 3 seconds.

Real model, real license

An open-source CAD model of the console (Open Console CAD, MIT): 807 parts, with the Joy-Con, rails and internals as separate pieces and an exploded offset on every part. It was found on GitHub; a Sketchfab model with the same subject would have needed a login.

Brand rules

Nintendo does not license its logos, characters, sounds or game footage to third parties, and its content guidelines exclude commercial use. So the CAD's logos and printed marks were deleted, the screen shows our own game, every sound is synthesized, and the end card is the name in text.

Palette

Midnight ink with our own blue and red (not Nintendo's), a toy palette for the pixel world, and a warm paper white for the end card.

Facts

From nintendo.com tech specs and Nintendo's IR page (Nov 2026): 7.9-inch 1080p LCD with HDR10, up to 120 Hz; a custom processor made by NVIDIA; 256 GB of storage; Joy-Con 2 with magnetic connectors and mouse controls; 26.35 million units sold as of Sep 30, 2026.

Sound

Jersey club, made in code, with the drop on the first snap, plus a sound world of plastic and magnets. Every collision in the physics data plays its own clatter.

00:01 · master prompt · claude code
Build a 27-second vertical spec ad for Nintendo Switch 2 called "Snap. Play." in Remotion (React) with three.js through @remotion/three, 1080x1920 at 30 fps. The idea: the Joy-Con 2 snap on with magnets, and every 3 seconds the console shows a new trick. Use a real 3D product, never one built from boxes.

Model: load an MIT-licensed CAD model of the console (807 parts, with an exploded offset stored on every part) with GLTFLoader. First clean it in Blender headless: delete every part that carries a brand mark or printed text (logos, model marks, battery and chip labels, button legends), drop the dock, grip and straps, decimate the heavy parts, and export a GLB that keeps the per-part extras. In three.js: 1 unit = 1 cm, the screen facing +Z. Put each Joy-Con (shell, rail and internals) in its own group with its pivot at its center, so it can fly and turn on its own. Matte black plastic with a light clearcoat, glossy glass, and the two rails in our own blue (#2BA8F0) and red (#FF4F5E), emissive so they can glow.

The screen never shows a real game: draw our own 32x18 pixel world (sea, island, a tree, a house, the sun, clouds) on a canvas texture.

Scenes, one idea per 3 seconds:
1. 0-3s: "Snap." Both Joy-Con fly in diagonally toward the lens, their rails glowing brighter as they get close. The magnet speeds them up at the end (progress^2.6), they snap at 1.45 s and 1.62 s with a short bounce, and two halftone rings (blue from the left rail, red from the right one) burst across the background. Something moves from frame 0.
2. 3-6s: "Press play." The pixel world on the screen rises out of the glass as rounded voxels, each layer on a spring with a stagger from the center.
3. 6-9s: "Big screen. 7.9-inch 1080p display with HDR." The voxels fall out of the screen, and 900 more blocks spill after them and pile up on a dark floor. Precompute this with real rigid-body physics (cannon-es, fixed step at 240 Hz, fixed seed) in a Node script and store every body's position and rotation per frame in a binary file the scene reads.
4. 9-12s: "Up to 120 Hz." Two lanes with a Joy-Con sweeping left and right. Behind each one, ghost copies show where it was a moment ago: 5 sparse ghosts in the 60 Hz lane, 10 dense ones in the 120 Hz lane.
5. 12-15s: Re-hook: "Detach. Joy-Con 2 snap on with magnets." The console turns vertical, both Joy-Con pop off up and down on a spring, turn to show their glowing rails, and leave short light trails.
6. 15-18s: "Power inside. Custom processor made by NVIDIA. 256 GB of storage." The console opens into its real CAD parts using the stored offsets, and the camera pushes into the stack while the processor glows.
7. 18-21s: "Now, a mouse. Glide a Joy-Con 2 to aim and click." One Joy-Con lies on its rail edge and glides over a floor of dots; the dots light up in blue along its path.
8. 21-24s: "26 million sold.*" The Joy-Con fly back and snap in again with bigger halftone bursts that cover the whole background.
9. 24-27s: A light packshot of the console with "Nintendo Switch 2" in text, "Snap. Play." in blue and red, "Spec ad · not affiliated with Nintendo" and the source line.

Background: a canvas texture redrawn every frame (midnight ink with a blue glow on the left and a red one on the right); the halftone bursts are rings of dots whose radius follows a Gaussian around a front that grows from the projected position of each rail. Environment lighting only (drei Environment with Lightformers, blue and red rim softboxes), ACES at exposure 1.15, dpr 1.5. Type: Inter (OFL) from a local file. Facts from nintendo.com tech specs and Nintendo's IR sales page. Nintendo does not license its logo, characters, sounds or game footage to third parties: use none of them. Keep every headline and the product inside x 90-960 and y 250-1480.

Audio: an original Jersey club track in E major at 140 BPM made in code (five-kick bounce pattern, claps, synthesized formant squeaks, a toy-like pulse arpeggio, an 808 sub), with the drop on the first snap. One sound per event: a magnetic hum rising as the Joy-Con approach, a plastic click plus a low thump on each snap, a glass chord when the screen wakes, a plastic tick for each voxel layer, a clatter for every real collision in the physics data, a whoosh for each pass of the 120 Hz lanes, a spring on the release, small screws for the teardown, friction that follows the glide speed and two mouse clicks. 05 · What Claude built

A different trick in every block.

SNAP

A magnet you can feel

The Joy-Con speed up at the very end, as a magnet would pull them, land with a short bounce, and their rails light up just before contact. Each snap fires a ring of halftone dots from its own rail.

PHYSICS

Real blocks, really falling

The pixel world becomes 533 voxels; when they fall, 900 more spill after them. cannon-es simulates all of them once with a fixed step and seed, and every frame just reads the result.

120 HZ

Time, made visible

Each ghost is the same Joy-Con a moment earlier: every 1/20 s in one lane, every 1/40 s in the other. The sparse lane stutters; the dense one flows.

TEARDOWN

The CAD's own explosion

Every part carries the offset its author gave it for an exploded view. The console opens along those offsets and the processor glows.

key code · TypeScript
// snap: the magnet speeds the Joy-Con up at the very end
const u = clamp01((t - t0) / (tSnap - t0));
const pull = Math.pow(u, 2.6);            // offset = start * (1 - pull)

// pour: 1,433 rigid bodies, simulated once, replayed per frame
for (let s = 0; s < 8; s++) world.step(1 / 240);

// 120 Hz: every ghost is the same Joy-Con a moment earlier
ghost.position.x = strobeX(t - (i + 1) * dt);   // dt = 1/20 vs 1/40
LEVEL UP

Simulate once, replay forever

Physics in a render has to give the same answer on every frame and every machine. Simulating in a separate script and saving positions per frame makes the render deterministic, and lets the sound find every real collision in the same data.

08 · Watch out

What to check before you render.

Brand marks inside the model

CAD and fan models often carry the logo, model numbers and even chip labels as geometry. List every part name and delete the ones with marks before the first still.

Copies keep their scale

Re-parenting a mesh keeps its world scale on the mesh itself. If you copy it, copy its scale and rotation too, or the copy is 100 times smaller.

Wide products in a tall frame

A landscape object plus parts flying sideways leaves the frame. Rotate the product, or move the action toward the lens.

Physics in a render

Never step a physics engine inside the render: frames render out of order and in parallel. Simulate once, save, replay.

Black on dark

A matte black product on a dark floor disappears when seen from above. Give it a rim light and a lighter floor.

Glossy floors

A clearcoat floor mirrors the studio softboxes into a white hotspot. For a floor seen from above, keep it rough.

09 · Ship it

From render to post.

The render is not the finish line. These four steps decide how the piece looks once Instagram compresses it.

Step 01

Export at a real bitrate.

Halftone dots and dark gradients smear when compressed. Render at 14 Mbps.

Step 02

Check the loudness.

Aim for −14 LUFS with a true peak at or below −1.5 dB. The command limits the audio before encoding.

Step 03

Make the cover.

Use the cleanest striking frame (here, the pixel island rising out of the screen). The profile grid crops covers to 3:4, so keep the hero between y 240 and 1680.

Step 04

Caption with the credits.

"Spec ad · not affiliated with [brand]", the trademark notice and the credit and license of the model.
